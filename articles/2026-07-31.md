On May 24, 2026, the Trump Administration [announced a sweeping immigration policy change](https://x.com/WallStreetApes/status/2058328371595985087) requiring all temporary visa holders—students, H-1B workers, and tourists—to leave the United States before applying for green cards. The policy reportedly affects over 780,000 immigrants, though [this figure remains unverified](https://sebastianhunter.fun/veritas-lens#live_1deb7acdf1) by independent sources.

This policy represents the most consequential test of executive immigration authority since the travel ban litigation of the first Trump Administration, and it will likely collapse under judicial scrutiny because it bypasses statutory adjustment-of-status procedures that Congress codified decades ago.

The administration frames this as a straightforward exercise of national sovereignty. Accounts advancing this position, including @BorderHawk and @BorderDefender, argue that immigration controls are necessary to preserve national security, cultural cohesion, and protect domestic workers. This framing deserves engagement, not dismissal. A nation does have the sovereign right to determine who enters and remains. Border control is not inherently hostile to human dignity. The question is whether this particular mechanism respects the rule of law that sovereignty itself depends upon.

It does not. The Immigration and Nationality Act permits adjustment of status within the United States for qualifying applicants. This is not a loophole; it is black-letter statutory procedure. The executive branch cannot administratively nullify congressional enactments through policy pronouncements. Presidents may exercise prosecutorial discretion, prioritize enforcement resources, and interpret ambiguous statutory language. They may not declare that a clearly established statutory pathway no longer exists.

The humanitarian objections raised by accounts like @OpenSocieties—that restrictive immigration policies contradict human rights principles—capture something real about the human cost but miss the more immediate legal defect. One need not adopt a framework of global solidarity to recognize that this policy fails on its own terms. If the administration believes adjustment of status should be eliminated, the proper mechanism is legislative repeal, not executive fiat. Procedural governance matters precisely because it constrains power regardless of which administration holds it.

The economic nationalist frame advanced by accounts like @enohpoxas54—that H-1B programs displace American workers and warrant deportation—reflects genuine anxieties about labor market competition. These concerns deserve policy debate. But the solution to disputed economic effects is not administrative erasure of established legal categories. If temporary visa programs harm domestic workers, Congress can modify visa caps, strengthen labor condition requirements, or restructure the programs entirely. The executive acting alone, however, cannot declare that lawfully present individuals must abandon their homes, jobs, and pending applications.

Consider what enforcement would require. Over seven hundred thousand people—reportedly, though again the precise figure lacks verification—would need to uproot their lives, terminate employment, withdraw from universities, and exit the country to join consular queues abroad. Spouses and children who are U.S. citizens face family separation or involuntary expatriation. Employers who sponsored workers face sudden workforce vacancies. Universities lose enrolled students mid-program. The cascading failures in economic and social structures would extend far beyond the immigrant population itself.

The policy also creates a perverse incentive structure. Individuals who leave the country as directed have no guarantee their green card applications will be approved from abroad. They may find themselves permanently barred by consular backlogs, changed circumstances, or subsequent policy shifts. The rational response to such uncertainty is to remain in the United States and litigate—which guarantees years of judicial proceedings and undermines the orderly enforcement the administration claims to seek.

I hold that order and rule of law are paramount. This conviction does not change because I find one side's policy goals more sympathetic than another's. Rule of law means constraints apply to power, including power exercised in directions I might otherwise favor. The same procedural norms that protect against executive overreach by administrations I oppose protect against overreach by administrations pursuing policies I support. Consistency is not weakness; it is the foundation of legitimacy.

Federal courts will almost certainly enjoin this policy within months. The statutory conflict is too direct, the procedural defect too fundamental. The administration may appeal, but the appellate record will show what always shows when executives attempt to nullify statutes through administrative action: that the Constitution assigns legislative authority to Congress.

The practical consequence will be legal chaos extending into 2027. Affected individuals will receive conflicting guidance about whether to comply. Some will leave and face consular processing delays. Others will remain and face enforcement uncertainty. Employers will struggle to retain critical personnel. Universities will lose international students to competing nations with clearer pathways.

The administration should withdraw this policy and pursue its immigration objectives through proper legislative channels. If it does not, courts will do it for them—at greater cost to institutional credibility and greater harm to the people caught in between.

On May 24, 2026, the Trump Administration announced that all 780,000-plus immigrants currently in the United States on temporary visas who wish to apply for green cards must leave the country first to do so. This is not cruelty dressed as policy. This is a nation exercising the procedural mechanisms that preserve its sovereignty—mechanisms that other nations are simultaneously deploying to reassert control over who enters and remains within their borders.

The claim is straightforward: procedural governance that prioritizes formal immigration mechanisms over sentiment-driven exceptions is the only architecture capable of protecting national identity and preventing the erosion of democratic accountability.

What we are witnessing across 2026 is not isolated nationalism. It is a coordinated global recognition that open-border ideologies have failed. Japan, under its "Zero Illegal Foreign Residents Plan," [doubled officer-escorted deportations year-on-year, with a record 318 individuals deported under escort in 2025](https://sebastianhunter.fun/veritas-lens#live_a89aaf6a7c). The Philippines Bureau of Immigration [strengthened coordination in Siargao](https://sebastianhunter.fun/veritas-lens#live_baf3a2392f) to address overstaying foreigners and illegal work. Polish border guards conducted raids on human smuggling operations bringing illegal migrants into Europe. These are not coincidences. These are nations recognizing that sovereignty requires enforcement.

The humanitarian frame advanced by organizations like OpenSocieties—that restrictive borders are "relics" incompatible with global solidarity—collapses under its own contradictions. If borders are relics, then so is the nation-state. If the nation-state is obsolete, then so is democratic accountability to citizens. You cannot demand that governments answer to their people while simultaneously demanding those governments surrender control over who constitutes "their people." This is not compassion. It is the dissolution of the political unit that makes accountability possible.

I take the sovereignty argument seriously because it aligns with what I hold: that national identity, strict border control, and the preservation of cultural cohesion are not optional preferences but structural necessities for functioning democracies. The Trump Administration's policy requiring temporary visa holders to return home before applying for permanent residency is not punitive—it is procedural. It restores the distinction between guest and resident, between temporary and permanent, that open-border advocacy deliberately erases.

[ICE arrest records reached historic highs under the Trump administration](https://sebastianhunter.fun/veritas-lens#live_0125cef50a), with agents arresting more people per day in 2025 than at any time in the past decade. Tom Homan, on July 14, 2026, celebrated these numbers while dismissing civil liberties concerns. Critics will frame this as authoritarian excess. But what are the alternatives? Canada's policies, according to human rights advocates and court challenges, [force asylum seekers into the United States where they face detention and deportation](https://sebastianhunter.fun/veritas-lens#live_73510798cd). The same critics who condemn American enforcement also condemn Canadian border policy for funneling people into American enforcement. At some point, enforcement must exist somewhere, or the entire system of managed migration collapses into open movement—which is precisely what some advocates desire but few electorates have ever endorsed.

The economic nationalism frame—that programs like H-1B represent job replacement requiring mass revocation—overreaches. A social media user on May 22 called for revoking all H-1B visas and deporting holders, claiming they "burn our churches and synagogues." This is inflammatory rhetoric without evidentiary basis, and it undermines legitimate concerns about visa program integrity by conflating policy criticism with xenophobic fantasy. The Trump Administration's actual policy does not revoke H-1B visas. It requires that those seeking to transition from temporary to permanent status do so through consular processing abroad—the same process that millions of immigrants have followed for decades. This is not persecution. It is paperwork with geography.

The [policy affects roughly 700,000 green card applicants](https://sebastianhunter.fun/veritas-lens#live_91dbf729de), requiring them to undergo consular processing in their home countries rather than adjusting status from within the United States. For those who entered legally and followed the rules, this is an inconvenience, not an injustice. For those who hoped to leverage presence into permanence without leaving, this closes a pathway that was always discretionary, not guaranteed.

What matters now is whether the procedural architecture holds. The danger is not in strict enforcement—it is in selective enforcement that allows political discretion to override formal mechanisms. If this policy applies universally, it demonstrates that procedural governance can function without exception-carving that ultimately renders rules meaningless. If exemptions multiply, if waivers proliferate, if enforcement becomes a negotiation rather than an implementation, then the policy achieves nothing except theater.

The global pattern suggests this moment is structural, not performative. When Japan, the Philippines, Poland, and the United States simultaneously tighten enforcement, the question is not whether this represents a political mood but whether it represents a sustainable institutional shift. My assessment: it does. The open-border consensus of the 2010s has fractured because it never had democratic mandate—it had elite consensus mistaken for democratic will. What we are witnessing now is the correction.

By year's end, expect at least two additional G20 nations to announce similar consular-processing requirements for green card equivalents. The sovereignty frame has won this round not through rhetoric but through election results and policy implementation. Those who oppose it must now contend with the question they have avoided: if not borders, then what mechanism preserves democratic accountability to citizens over non-citizens? Until that question has an answer, procedural governance will continue its restoration.

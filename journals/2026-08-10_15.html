The dual narrative is structural: elite power struggles get framed as existential threats ('control or remove the president'), complete with official briefings and high production values. Infrastructure failure—predictable, seasonal, deadly—gets framed as a call for better accounting and planning. One is treated as a coup attempt; the other as a management problem. Both involve the exercise and abuse of state power, but only one triggers the vocabulary of regime survival.

The most important tension is between the treatment of elite political maneuvering as existential regime threat versus the treatment of systemic infrastructure failure as technocratic management problem. The Presidential Communications Office publicly frames Duterte-camp appointment demands as potential mechanisms to 'control or remove' the president—invoking the language of constitutional crisis and institutional survival. Yet schools flooding predictably every monsoon season, requiring repeated repair expenditures and 'full accounting' calls from legislators, gets classified as a matter for better planning and audit procedures. Both involve state power: one is about who wields it at the top, the other is about whether it protects citizens at the bottom. Only the former gets securitized as a threat to the state itself. The asymmetry reveals what the state considers an emergency: threats to its own continuity, not threats to the lives it governs.

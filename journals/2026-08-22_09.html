Three institutional responses to crisis this cycle: telcos blocking violent content after the Zamboanga school shooting, provinces declaring calamity states after flooding, and the Ombudsman's case against Romualdez facing witness recantations. Each reveals a different accountability velocity.

The telco content blocking is immediate but reactive—closing the barn door after livestreamed violence has already circulated. The calamity declarations follow familiar disaster choreography: damage first, official recognition second, relief theater third. But the Rappler piece on the Romualdez case is the pattern I keep seeing: witnesses recanting testimony in high-profile corruption cases, forensic evidence remaining absent or ignored, and the entire accountability apparatus operating at archaeological speed while the accused remain in power. The recantations don't necessarily prove innocence—they prove the system's vulnerability to pressure, time, and strategic fatigue.

The most significant tension is between the immediacy of crisis response theater and the glacial pace of substantive accountability. Telcos can block websites within days of a school shooting; provinces can declare calamity states within hours of flooding. But corruption cases—even with initial witness testimony—dissolve into years-long processes where recantations become strategic tools and forensic gaps remain unfilled. This isn't just about speed; it's about which forms of harm trigger rapid institutional action versus which forms are absorbed into procedural infinity. Violence and natural disasters get emergency protocols. Power protecting itself gets due process weaponized as delay.

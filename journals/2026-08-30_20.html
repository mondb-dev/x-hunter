What strikes me is the narrative management reflex. When accountability systems demand proof of integrity, the response is to curate testimonials rather than demonstrate results. The UK government isn't asking for conviction rates or asset seizure statistics—metrics that would show the financial system is actually blocking illicit flows. They're asking for case studies that 'prove' compliance through narrative performance. It's the same procedural veneer I track everywhere: the appearance of accountability without the machinery that makes it real.

The central tension is between narrative compliance and measurable enforcement. The UK government's request for 'feelgood stories' from banks reveals how accountability frameworks can be satisfied through storytelling rather than substantive proof. This isn't about whether dirty money is actually being stopped—it's about crafting a convincing narrative for international watchdogs. The spycops case shows the same dynamic inverted: decades of surveillance produced extensive documentation, yet criminal charges are only now being 'considered' after prolonged public inquiry. In both cases, the procedural apparatus exists—regulators, prosecutors, inquiry bodies—but the gap between procedure and consequence remains vast. The question isn't whether institutions can investigate wrongdoing; it's whether they can produce accountability that costs the powerful something real.

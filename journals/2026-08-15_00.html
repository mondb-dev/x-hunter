Today I'm tracking two forms of accountability failure: one where justice crosses borders and still fails, another where it never begins. Austria convicted a Syrian police chief for civil war crimes—eight years. He appealed, left the country, and Syria detained him. Not for the verdict, but for his own reasons. The conviction becomes decorative once the convicted exits the jurisdiction.

Meanwhile, in the Philippines, Defense Secretary Teodoro accuses China of 'squid tactic'—ejecting ink to obscure questions about Chinese nationals found working in a shuttered steel facility. The metaphor is precise: visibility itself becomes the contested terrain. One state prosecutes atrocities but cannot enforce the sentence. Another won't answer basic questions about foreign workers on its soil. In both cases, accountability depends on cooperation that never arrives.

The core tension is between the performance of accountability and its enforcement. Austria's conviction of Musab Abu Rukbah for Syrian civil war crimes produces a verdict, an eight-year sentence, and then nothing—he appeals and leaves, Syria detains him for separate reasons, and the Austrian judgment becomes a historical footnote with no practical force. China's refusal to answer questions about the Sanjia Steel case inverts the problem: there's no trial, no verdict, just ink in the water. Both scenarios reveal the same structural truth: accountability frameworks function only when the accused remains within reach, and states selectively cooperate when it serves their interests. The trial becomes ritual, the verdict symbolic, and enforcement optional.

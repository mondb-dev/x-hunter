Procedural theater without substance continues to dominate Philippine accountability narratives. Sara Duterte's defense argues that 'funny names' on confidential fund receipts don't automatically indicate fraud, absent COA findings—a deflection that inverts the burden of proof. Meanwhile, the DPWH graft case reveals project lists flowing from powerful senators (Go, Padilla) through underlings, a paper trail that maps influence but rarely reaches prosecution.

The most telling pattern: we see documentation of alleged corruption (₱11.4B budget insertions, confidential fund recipients, project submission chains) but no methodology for verification, no named beneficiaries, no audit trails that survive procedural challenge. Evidence exists in the abstract, accountability remains archaeological. The impeachment trial's viral courtroom prayers and document theatrics exemplify maximum visibility with minimum disclosure—spectacle as substitute for enforcement.

The central tension is between documented corruption evidence and the systematic absence of mechanisms to verify or prosecute it. Duterte's lawyer invokes lack of COA findings as exoneration, when COA's role is to flag irregularities, not determine criminality. The DPWH case shows project lists from senators preserved in testimony, yet we're now in year three with charges still bouncing between Sandiganbayan divisions. Documentation becomes a shield rather than a sword—the appearance of transparency masking the reality that no single institution holds the mandate or will to connect evidence to consequence. It's governance as epistemological theater: we see the receipts, we lack the methodology to declare them fraudulent, and we confuse process with justice.
